The IETF is for Everyone: Toward Inclusive and Equitable Participation in Internet Governance

Abstract

This document aims to foster a deeper reflection within the IETF community on inclusive participation, equitable access, and the implications of global meeting venue selections on diverse contributors. It seeks to complement existing RFCs by proposing additional dialogue, tools, and evaluation mechanisms, while also highlighting the shared responsibility of underrepresented regions in mobilizing local stakeholders to engage with the IETF. This draft includes concrete proposals, indicative metrics, and an implementation roadmap to move from discussion to action.¶

1. Introduction

The Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF) has long promoted principles of openness, inclusivity, and technical excellence. As the global Internet landscape evolves, so too must our mechanisms for ensuring equitable participation.¶

This document responds to growing calls for reflection on how the IETF addresses systemic and structural barriers that affect contributors from underrepresented regions and communities. Building on existing frameworks and policies, in particular [RFC7704], [RFC8718], [RFC8719], and [RFC9712], this draft outlines community-driven proposals to foster greater inclusion in practice.¶

In particular, it highlights three underexplored dimensions: (1) the lack of academic recognition and incentives for contributors; (2) the recurring concerns around meeting venue accessibility, safety, and equity; and (3) the need for targeted grassroots engagement, especially in Africa and other underserved regions, to build long-term pipelines for contribution.¶

Through collaborative strategies such as mentorship, multilingual onboarding, university engagement, and periodic community consultations (e.g., the Africa Internet Governance Forum), this draft invites discussion on how the IETF can better align its practices with its foundational commitments to openness and global reach.¶

Importantly, this document also emphasizes that inclusion is a shared responsibility. While the IETF must lower barriers and facilitate access, communities in underrepresented regions must also proactively mobilize youth, academia, policymakers, and local technical communities to engage with and contribute to the IETF's work.¶

2. Current Participation Metrics and Gaps

Despite ongoing efforts, participation in IETF activities remains uneven across global regions. The publicly available indicators, although partial, point in the same direction:¶

  • In the 2024 IETF Community Survey, 2.5% of respondents were based in Africa, compared with 40.4% in Europe and 39.4% in North America [IETF-AR-2024]. Survey respondents are not identical to meeting participants, but no other region-level series is published.¶
  • An analysis of author contact details found 15 RFCs with at least one Africa-based author out of more than 9,000 RFCs published as of January 2022, i.e., well under 1% [RIA-2022].¶
  • To date, no IETF plenary meeting has been held in Africa. Under the current meeting policy, meetings rotate primarily among North America, Europe, and Asia, with other regions reachable only through "exploratory" meetings [RFC8719] [RFC9712].¶

Several indicators that matter for inclusion are not currently published in a form that allows regional comparison, notably visa refusal rates for registered participants and the regional distribution of working group chairs and other leadership roles. Earlier revisions of this document cited estimates for these; they have been removed pending verifiable data. Establishing these baselines is itself one of the proposals of this document (see Section 9.11).¶

These indicators highlight structural imbalances that require targeted interventions to ensure that the IETF's technical standards reflect truly global perspectives and use cases.¶

3. Motivations and Context

This draft was inspired by feedback from various IETF participants and observations across recent meetings. It acknowledges that while [RFC7704], [RFC8718], and [RFC9712] lay a strong foundation, practical issues of access, safety, and diversity persist. Community insights were also gathered during Africa IGF 2025 discussions, where similar challenges were voiced by participants across the continent.¶

A recurring theme is the need to rebalance efforts: while the IETF should continue improving its processes, regions with lower participation must also develop local strategies to identify, mentor, and sponsor new contributors, and advocate for the relevance of IETF work within their own governance and academic ecosystems.¶

4. Alignment with Global Digital Governance Frameworks

Promoting inclusive participation in Internet standards development is not merely an ethical imperative but aligns with multiple global digital governance frameworks and commitments:¶

  • UN Sustainable Development Goal 9: Build resilient infrastructure, promote inclusive and sustainable industrialization and foster innovation.¶
  • ITU's Connect 2030 Agenda: Leave no one offline, with a specific focus on bridging digital divides.¶
  • African Union Digital Transformation Strategy 2020-2030: Calls for meaningful African participation in global Internet governance.¶
  • Internet Society's 2025 Action Plan: Commits to an Internet for Everyone through inclusive governance.¶
  • UN Secretary-General's Roadmap for Digital Cooperation: Emphasizes inclusive multistakeholder participation, including in technical standards.¶

By addressing participation gaps, the IETF contributes to these broader global development objectives while strengthening the legitimacy and relevance of its technical standards.¶

5. Voices from the Community (Synthesis of Regional Discussions)

This section reflects recurring themes and concerns raised during community discussions held in various regional and global forums, including Africa IGF sessions, Internet Society chapter meetings, and informal technical community exchanges. It is a qualitative synthesis based on the author's participation and notes; it is not the result of a systematic survey and should not be read as statistically representative. These perspectives nevertheless illustrate structural barriers to participation and highlight opportunities for reform. They are consistent with the barriers identified in independent research on African contributions to standards development [RIA-2022].¶

Participants from academic and technical communities across Africa consistently emphasized that sustained participation in the IETF remains constrained by a combination of structural factors. These include visa-related uncertainties, high travel and accommodation costs, and time-zone misalignment, all of which disproportionately affect contributors based in the Global South. As a result, individuals with relevant technical expertise often lack viable pathways to maintain long-term engagement in standards development processes.¶

Several discussions highlighted the catalytic effect that hosting IETF meetings in underrepresented regions could have on local academic and technical ecosystems. Beyond immediate participation gains, such events were described as having the potential to create visible role models, strengthen institutional engagement, and inspire future generations of engineers and policymakers to engage in Internet standards work.¶

Community members also repeatedly underscored the importance of informal, in-person interactions, often referred to as the "hallway track", in building trust, collaboration, and consensus within the IETF. The inability to attend meetings physically, whether due to financial constraints or mobility restrictions, was widely perceived as limiting access to these critical aspects of participation, even when remote tools are available.¶

Finally, academic participants noted a systemic disconnect between Internet standards contributions and university incentive structures. In several discussions, it was observed that the absence of formal recognition of standards work (such as RFC authorship) in academic promotion and evaluation systems significantly reduces incentives for faculty members in developing regions to contribute to IETF processes.¶

6. Community Feedback and Observations

This section summarizes the key feedback received through consultations, mailing-list and side discussions, and direct engagement:¶

  • The challenge of travel barriers (visas, safety, cost).¶
  • Limited regional rotation (no IETF meetings in Africa to date).¶
  • Importance of the hallway track and in-person connections.¶
  • Barriers to mentorship and project engagement beyond venue issues.¶
  • The need for local communities to take ownership of IETF outreach and capacity-building, rather than waiting for invitations or funding from the Global North.¶
  • Lack of academic recognition disincentivizes participation from university researchers.¶
  • Time-zone challenges for real-time participation in working groups.¶
  • Limited awareness of IETF processes and opportunities in underrepresented regions.¶

7. Addressing Common Concerns

When discussing greater inclusion, several objections are frequently raised. This section addresses these concerns with practical responses:¶

  • "Logistical challenges are too great for hosting meetings in new regions": The current policy already provides for exploratory meetings outside the three main regions, and [RFC9712] allows the IETF Administration LLC to schedule them through its normal procedures. Partnerships with local Internet communities and existing regional event infrastructure (e.g., the African Internet Summit, AfNOG, AfPIF) can reduce risk. Several African cities now regularly host large international events.¶
  • "Quality of contributions may decline with broader participation": Broader participation brings more deployment contexts and edge cases into the review process. IETF standards are adopted through rough consensus and running code, so additional participants add review capacity without lowering the bar for adoption.¶
  • "Cost is prohibitive for expanded participation programs": Options include a dedicated inclusion budget line, better use of hybrid participation tools to reduce travel needs, and partnerships with regional development institutions and foundations focused on digital inclusion.¶
  • "We already have remote participation options": While remote tools exist, they do not replicate the serendipitous collaboration of hallway tracks, informal mentoring, and relationship building that drives IETF work. A blended approach is needed.¶
  • "Standards are technical, not political": While technical excellence remains paramount, the selection of which problems to solve, which use cases to prioritize, and whose perspectives shape solutions inevitably involves considerations of representation and equity.¶

8. Communicating the Value Proposition

Framing inclusion as a strategic advantage rather than a compliance requirement can build broader support within the IETF community:¶

  • Technical Robustness: More diverse perspectives are likely to surface more edge cases and deployment challenges, leading to more resilient standards.¶
  • Market Relevance: Standards that work effectively in constrained contexts (bandwidth limits, intermittent connectivity, diverse regulatory environments) tend to work well everywhere.¶
  • Legitimacy and Adoption: Standards developed with meaningful input from all regions are better positioned for broad adoption and implementation.¶
  • Innovation Catalyst: Constraints in emerging markets (limited bandwidth, high latency, intermittent power) drive creative solutions that can benefit all Internet users.¶
  • Future-Proofing: Much of the growth in Internet users is expected to come from currently underrepresented regions; their needs and contexts should inform today's standards.¶
  • Risk Mitigation: Homogeneous standards development risks blind spots; diversity of perspective builds resilience.¶

9. Proposed Directions

9.1. Initiate Community Dialogues on Venue Inclusivity

Encourage structured discussions on how the IETF selects and rotates its venues, integrating considerations of safety, inclusion, and representation, especially as conditions evolve between selection and meeting time. Explore a transparent scoring approach for potential venues that includes accessibility metrics, visa accessibility for diverse nationalities, and local community engagement plans, building on the requirements of [RFC8718] as updated by [RFC9712].¶

9.2. Develop Operational Tools for Assessment

Propose tools, scorecards, or collaborative reviews to assess venues and host countries based on inclusiveness, accessibility, and risk of exclusion. A standardized assessment framework could be applied consistently across potential meeting locations, including factors such as visa accessibility for Global South participants, physical accessibility, gender safety, and cost of accommodation.¶

9.3. Support Regional Engagement and Rotations

Explore concrete mechanisms to hold an exploratory meeting in an underrepresented region (e.g., Africa) under the "1-1-1-*" policy of [RFC8719] and the scheduling process introduced by [RFC9712], including logistical partnerships, travel funds, and hybrid session enhancements. In the longer term, consider whether every major world region should host an IETF meeting at least once every 5-7 years.¶

9.3.1. Transformative Impact of a Meeting in an Underrepresented Region

Holding an IETF meeting in Africa or another underrepresented region would serve as more than a logistical event; it would act as a catalyst for awareness and ecosystem development. Such a meeting could:¶

  • Raise visibility of the IETF among local policymakers, regulators, academia, and the private sector, demystifying the standards development process.¶
  • Stimulate academic interest, leading to curriculum integration and research partnerships between local universities and IETF participants.¶
  • Create a lasting legacy through local mirror events, mentorship networks, and continued collaboration with IETF working groups.¶
  • Send a strong signal that the IETF is committed to being a truly global standards body.¶
  • Generate local media coverage and public awareness about Internet governance and technical standards, inspiring the next generation of contributors.¶
  • Build local organizational capacity for hosting major technical events, creating positive spillover effects for the regional Internet community.¶

9.4. Academic Recognition Pathways

To bridge the academia-IETF divide and create sustainable participation pipelines:¶

  • RFCs as scholarly publications: RFCs undergo public review that has been argued to be comparable to academic peer review, and it has been proposed that they be treated as scholarly publications [Carpenter2010]. Universities could be encouraged to take RFCs and substantive IETF contributions into account in hiring and promotion. Guidelines for academic institutions on evaluating IETF contributions could be developed.¶
  • University liaison program: Create formal roles for computer science departments to co-develop standards. Appoint IETF ambassadors at universities in underrepresented regions to facilitate engagement.¶
  • Curriculum integration: Partner with university networks such as the Association of African Universities (https://www.aau.org/) to offer academic credit for IETF contributions. Develop teaching modules on Internet standards for undergraduate and graduate programs.¶
  • Local academic advocacy: Encourage universities in underrepresented regions to formally recognize IETF participation in promotion, tenure, and funding decisions. Create model promotion criteria that value standards contributions.¶
  • Research partnerships: Facilitate joint research initiatives between IETF participants and academic institutions, including through the IRTF, particularly on topics relevant to emerging markets.¶

9.5. Enhanced Grassroots Engagement and Youth Involvement

Foster collaboration with universities, local Internet communities, and grassroots organizations to demystify IETF processes, support mentorship programs, and identify new contributors from underrepresented regions. Such efforts should build on, rather than duplicate, existing programs such as the Internet Society's Policymaker Program to the IETF [ISOC-PMP] and the IETF Education and Outreach Directorate [EODIR].¶

For example, for African participation:¶

  • IETF mirror events at African universities with hybrid participation support, allowing local students to experience IETF processes without travel.¶
  • Mobilizing Internet Society local chapters to involve youth and academia in IETF work through dedicated outreach programs.¶
  • Mentorship pipelines connecting academia to working groups, pairing experienced IETF participants with newcomers from underrepresented regions.¶
  • Local "IETF awareness campaigns" led by chapters, universities, and regulators to promote the value of participation and demystify the standards process.¶
  • Student travel grants specifically for participants from underrepresented regions, with mentorship components to ensure meaningful engagement.¶
  • Regional IETF preparatory workshops before major meetings to onboard new participants and help them navigate IETF processes effectively.¶

9.6. Advance Multilingual and Accessible Communication

Consider translating key onboarding materials and IETF resources into additional languages to support broader global accessibility and comprehension. Specific actions include:¶

  • Translate the Tao of the IETF and key orientation materials into French, Spanish, Arabic, Portuguese, and other major languages.¶
  • Explore live interpretation or captioning in multiple languages for plenary sessions, starting with hybrid meetings.¶
  • Develop visual guides and video tutorials explaining IETF processes for non-native English speakers.¶
  • Create a glossary of IETF terminology in multiple languages to lower the initial learning curve.¶
  • Establish a buddy system pairing non-native English speakers with experienced participants for document review and presentation practice.¶

9.7. Shared Responsibility and Multistakeholder Advocacy

Achieving equitable participation requires advocacy and action at all levels of Internet governance. Local stakeholders in underrepresented regions should:¶

  • Advocate within regional IGFs (e.g., Africa IGF, LACIGF) to include IETF participation as a standing agenda item, creating regular touchpoints between the IETF and regional communities.¶
  • Engage national regulators and ministries to facilitate visas, provide travel grants, and recognize IETF contributions in national digital strategies and human capital development plans.¶
  • Organize local contribution workshops to train new contributors on how to engage with Internet-Draft development and working groups, building local capacity for sustained participation.¶
  • Build partnerships between universities, ISOC chapters, and local industry to sponsor and mentor potential IETF participants, creating sustainable support ecosystems.¶
  • Promote the narrative that contributing to global Internet standards is a matter of digital sovereignty and technical self-determination.¶
  • Establish national IETF contact points within regulatory agencies and academic networks to coordinate participation efforts and share information.¶

The IETF, in turn, could recognize and support these bottom-up initiatives through liaison programs, seed funding, and formal channels for regional input into meeting planning and policy development. This could include a dedicated budget line for global inclusion initiatives, decided by the IETF Administration LLC [RFC8711], and an advisory mechanism with diverse regional representation.¶

9.8. Institutional Advocacy and Partnerships

Grassroots mobilization (Section 9.7) can be amplified by regional and international organizations whose mandates already cover digital development, higher education, Internet infrastructure, or Internet governance in Africa. This document encourages advocacy toward, and partnerships with, such organizations, in particular:¶

  • African Union Commission (AUC): Recognize participation in open Internet standards development as a component of the implementation of the Digital Transformation Strategy for Africa, and encourage Member States to reflect it in national digital strategies, skills programs, and research funding.¶
  • United Nations Economic Commission for Africa (UNECA): As host of the Africa IGF secretariat, support a recurring session on Internet standards participation in the Africa IGF programme and in national and sub-regional IGFs, and contribute research and data on African participation in standards development organizations.¶
  • UNESCO: Drawing on its mandates in higher education, science, and multilingualism, and on its Internet Universality (ROAM-X) indicators [UNESCO-ROAMX], encourage national assessments to consider participation in open, multistakeholder standards processes, support academic recognition of standards contributions, and support the translation of onboarding materials.¶
  • AFRINIC: As the Regional Internet Registry for Africa, and together with regional technical events such as the African Internet Summit, provide a natural venue for pre-IETF preparatory workshops, newcomer onboarding, and identification of technical contributors from its membership, within the limits of its institutional capacity.¶
  • Internet Society (ISOC): Building on its existing Policymaker Program and fellowship activities [ISOC-PMP], and on its network of African chapters, expand IETF awareness and onboarding activities in Africa and coordinate with the pilot proposed in Section 9.9.¶

Other organizations could usefully be involved, for example African research and education networks (such as WACREN, UbuntuNet Alliance, and ASREN), AfNOG, Smart Africa, and the African Telecommunications Union.¶

Such engagement aims at awareness, capacity building, recognition, and support for individual participation. It does not imply any role for these organizations in IETF decision-making, which remains open to all and based on the participation of individuals and on rough consensus [RFC3935]. Advocacy should therefore focus on enabling people from the region to participate directly in IETF work, rather than on institutional representation.¶

9.9. Proposed Pilot: IETF Africa Engagement Program

To move from discussion to action, this document proposes a 2-year pilot program focused on African engagement, designed in coordination with existing Internet Society and IETF programs:¶

  • Africa Fellowship Track: A number of fully funded places per IETF meeting for participants from African institutions, including travel, accommodation, and mentorship support (an indicative target of 20 per meeting).¶
  • Regional Hub Coordination: Designated liaison contacts in North, West, East, Central, and Southern Africa to coordinate local engagement, identify potential contributors, and provide regional context to IETF processes.¶
  • Academic Partnership Pilot: Formal partnerships with a small number of African universities (indicatively five) for curriculum integration, joint research, and faculty engagement on IETF-relevant topics.¶
  • Pre-IETF Technical Deep-Dive: Regional technical workshops before IETF meetings to help new participants understand agenda items, prepare contributions, and build confidence for active participation.¶
  • Local Mirror Event Program: Support for 3-5 local IETF mirror events per year across Africa, with hybrid connections to main meetings and dedicated session facilitators.¶
  • Documentation and Evaluation: Documentation of the pilot program with regular progress reports and a final evaluation to inform scaling to other regions.¶

9.10. Proposed: A Time-Bound Global Inclusion Effort

To provide structured coordination of inclusion efforts, this document proposes a lightweight, time-bound effort. Its exact form (for example, a working group in the General Area, a directorate, or an IESG-appointed design team) should be determined through the usual IETF processes, starting with a discussion in GENDISPATCH and, if appropriate, a BoF:¶

  • Charter: A 12-month mandate to develop actionable recommendations and implementation plans for IETF inclusion initiatives.¶
  • Representation: Active participation from all global regions, with deliberate outreach to underrepresented regions.¶
  • Deliverables: (1) proposed inclusion criteria for venue selection, for consideration under [RFC8718]; (2) a design for a sustainable fellowship program; (3) a framework for academic recognition of IETF contributions; (4) a proposal for publishing regional participation baselines.¶
  • Governance: Accountable to the IESG for process matters, with coordination with the IETF Administration LLC on budget, meetings, and staff support.¶
  • Resources: Staff support and a modest budget for community consultations and outreach.¶
  • Success Metrics: Clear metrics for evaluating the effort's impact on participation diversity.¶

9.11. Proposed Success Metrics

Measurable progress is essential for evaluating the effectiveness of inclusion initiatives. The following table proposes indicative targets. The first step is to agree on definitions and publish baselines; the targets are intended to stimulate discussion, not as commitments.¶

10. Immediate Next Steps

This document invites concrete actions from various stakeholders within the IETF ecosystem:¶

  1. Community: Discuss this document in GENDISPATCH and related venues, and identify the appropriate home for the proposals it contains.¶
  2. IESG and IAB: Consider a statement of commitment to equitable participation and allocate time for periodic inclusion progress reviews.¶
  3. IETF Administration LLC: Assess the feasibility of an exploratory meeting in Africa under [RFC9712], and consider a dedicated budget line for participation support from underrepresented regions in a forthcoming budget cycle.¶
  4. IETF Administration LLC and Secretariat: Publish regional participation baselines and pilot the proposed venue assessment tools in a future meeting planning cycle.¶
  5. Working Group Chairs: Consider mentoring at least one new contributor from an underrepresented region within their working groups.¶
  6. Regional Stakeholders: Form national IETF contact groups to coordinate local engagement and advocacy efforts.¶
  7. Internet Society Chapters: Launch local IETF awareness campaigns and identify potential contributors for mentorship.¶
  8. Academic Partners: Begin curriculum integration pilots in a small number of universities per region.¶
  9. Corporate Participants: Sponsor fellowship participants from underrepresented regions.¶
  10. Regional and International Organizations (e.g., AUC, UNECA, UNESCO, AFRINIC, ISOC): Include IETF participation in their capacity-building, higher-education, and Internet governance programmes, as described in Section 9.8.¶
  11. Community at Large: Participate in the proposed dialogues on venue inclusivity and provide feedback on implementation proposals.¶

11. Illustrative Initiatives: Bringing the IETF to African and Global South Forums

One practical illustration of these approaches is a workshop proposal submitted to the Internet Governance Forum (IGF) 2026 entitled "Standards for All: Bringing Global South Academia to IETF".¶

The proposed session aims to explore how universities, research institutions, Internet governance communities, standards organizations, and technical communities can collaborate to improve awareness of Internet standards development and encourage broader participation in the IETF.¶

A second illustration is the session "Africa at the Standards Table: Why Participation in the IETF Matters", scheduled at the Africa Internet Governance Forum 2026 in Accra, Ghana, on 3 November 2026. The session brings together IETF leadership, African IETF contributors, and regional Internet governance actors to discuss participation trends, the value of stronger African engagement, and practical steps to expand Africa's contribution to Internet standards development. Outcomes of that discussion are expected to inform a future revision of this document.¶

Preliminary discussions related to these initiatives have involved participants from different stakeholder communities, including representatives from organizations active in Internet governance, technical coordination, education, and capacity development. These discussions remain exploratory and should not be interpreted as formal institutional endorsements or commitments.¶

12. The IETF Participation Continuum

Participation in Internet standards development is rarely the result of a single intervention. Instead, it generally emerges through a progressive process involving awareness, learning, initial engagement, contribution, and community integration.¶

This document proposes considering inclusion in the IETF through a participation continuum composed of complementary stages:¶

  1. Awareness: understanding what the IETF is, how Internet standards are developed, and how individuals can participate.¶
  2. Education and Training: providing accessible learning opportunities on Internet standards and IETF processes.¶
  3. First Participation: enabling individuals to attend IETF meetings, participate remotely, join discussions, and interact with contributors.¶
  4. Mentoring and Community Support: helping newcomers navigate technical discussions and contribution processes.¶
  5. Technical Contributions: encouraging participation in working groups, reviews, and Internet-Draft development.¶
  6. RFC Authorship and Community Leadership: supporting contributors toward authorship and leadership roles.¶

Each stage reinforces the next. Expanding awareness increases the number of potential contributors. Education creates understanding. Mentoring facilitates effective participation. Sustained engagement can ultimately lead to broader representation among contributors and community leaders. The proposals in Section 9 can be read as interventions at one or more of these stages, and the metrics in Section 9.11 as a way to observe progression between them.¶

13. IETF Awareness as a Prerequisite for Inclusion

Many discussions on improving participation in the IETF focus on reducing barriers faced by individuals who are already aware of the organization and its processes. However, discussions within underrepresented communities suggest that another challenge often precedes participation itself: awareness.¶

A significant number of students, researchers, engineers, policymakers, and Internet practitioners remain unfamiliar with the IETF, its open participation model, and the process through which Internet-Drafts evolve into RFCs.¶

Improving awareness should therefore be considered a fundamental component of inclusion. Introducing Internet standards development concepts into academic programmes, research initiatives, technical training activities, and Internet governance discussions can help create sustainable pathways for future contributors.¶

Such awareness-building activities complement existing IETF and Internet Society outreach mechanisms by expanding the pool of individuals who may eventually engage in working groups, contribute to Internet-Drafts, and participate in the broader standards development ecosystem.¶

14. Conclusion

This document does not propose immediate policy changes but instead seeks to foster thoughtful community reflection and encourage collaborative exploration of solutions that support the IETF's inclusivity goals. The proposals presented represent a comprehensive approach to addressing participation gaps through structural changes, targeted programs, and shared responsibility.¶

Through proposals on academic recognition, grassroots engagement, venue selection dialogue, multilingual participation, impact metrics, and shared responsibility, this draft aims to offer constructive directions grounded in community input and practical experience. The included pilot program, coordination proposal, and indicative metrics provide a pathway from discussion to implementation.¶

By broadening participation and addressing structural imbalances, the IETF can continue to evolve as a truly global, open, and equitable standards body. The technical robustness, relevance, and legitimacy of IETF standards will be strengthened through more diverse participation, ultimately benefiting all Internet users worldwide.¶

Community feedback is warmly invited to refine, challenge, or build upon these directions. This document should serve as a starting point for constructive dialogue and decisive action toward a more inclusive IETF.¶

15. Security Considerations

This document has no direct security implications for Internet protocols. Proposals that involve collecting participation data, in particular data on visa outcomes, raise privacy considerations; such data should be collected on a voluntary basis, minimized, aggregated before publication, and protected in line with the IETF's privacy practices.¶

Appendix A. Draft Implementation Roadmap

This appendix outlines an indicative sequence for implementing the proposals in this document. Phases are expressed relative to the start of community discussion rather than as fixed calendar dates.¶

  1. Phase 1 (months 0-6): Community discussion of this draft in GENDISPATCH and regional forums; agreement on the form of the coordination effort; publication of first participation baselines; translation of key onboarding materials into three languages.¶
  2. Phase 2 (months 6-12): Coordination effort begins work; development of venue assessment tools; launch of academic recognition advocacy; first regional preparatory workshop held; initial partnerships with African universities established.¶
  3. Phase 3 (months 12-18): First cohort of the Africa fellowship track; academic recognition guidelines published; feasibility assessment of an exploratory meeting in Africa completed; first local mirror events supported.¶
  4. Phase 4 (months 18-24): Evaluation of pilot programs; adjustments based on lessons learned; decision on scaling to additional regions.¶
  5. Beyond 24 months: Implementation of successful pilot elements; ongoing metric tracking and reporting.¶
